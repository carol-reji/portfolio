01

Starting from a blank sheet

Longhorn Baja is a first-year team. No previous car to measure against, no inherited CAD, no documented architecture, no BOM. When I took steering there was a chassis, a rulebook, two drivers and an empty assembly file.

So the deliverable was never just a steering rack. It was the first version of a subsystem the team iterates on for years — which means every number needs a reason written next to it, or the next lead inherits the same blank sheet I did. That is why the calculation set is 30 pages and why the failed versions are still in it.

04

The calculation that killed my first design

I started where a first-year team starts: a solid round shaft, 0.63″ diameter, sized off a driver input of 150 lbf at a 4.92″ wheel radius — 738 lbf·in of steering torque. Stress-wise it passed comfortably. Then I ran angle of twist.

Shigley's acceptable bands are 0.03–0.17° for a stiff column and 1–3° for typical structure. Past 5° the driver turns the wheel and nothing happens at the tires for the first several degrees. At 7.92° the design was safe and useless — it would not have broken, it would have felt broken.

This is the calculation I'd hand a recruiter first. Not because it passed — because it failed, and it failed on the criterion the driver actually feels rather than the one that shows up in a stress plot.
05

Fix: put the material where torsion lives

Twist is inversely proportional to polar moment of area, and J is driven by the fourth power of radius — so the material near the centreline is dead weight in torsion. Moving from the solid 0.63″ shaft to a 0.625″ ID × 1.125″ OD hollow tube multiplied J by ×9.7, raised torsional stiffness ×11.4 and cut twist by 91% — from 7.92° into the band a driver reads as direct, without a heavier column.

Material went AISI 1020 low-carbon steel first per Shigley's ranking, with 4130/4140 held in reserve if it yielded — it doesn't. Column supports were placed off a cantilever model, and the bushing sits as close to the steering wheel as the packaging allows, because that is the span that dominates deflection at the driver's hands.

06

Deleting both U-joints

Ergonomics fixed the steering-wheel position, the chassis fixed the rack, and between them sat 35.4° of misalignment. The obvious answer is two U-joints at 17.7° each, so that is where I started — sizing them and checking operating angles against Borgeson's published data, including the rule that paired joints must be phased so their velocity ripple cancels.

ITER 1
One U-joint
Cannot take the full angle without running the joint past a sane operating angle. Binding risk, and per the FSAE upper-steering literature the difference between one- and two-joint behaviour is significant enough not to hand-wave.
ITER 2
Two U-joints, 17.7° each, phased
Geometrically fine on paper. In our shop: non-uniform angular velocity if the phasing is off by a spline tooth, wobble and vibration straight into the driver's hands, four extra machined interfaces to get right, and two more parts to buy, fit and inspect.
CHOSEN
Zero U-joints — rotate the rack 35.4° instead
The misalignment did not have to live in the column. Rotating the NARRco rack to meet the column removed both joints: no velocity ripple, no phasing to get wrong, no binding, fewer parts, and a straight shot the team can actually manufacture. The rack also runs inverted — mounted the conventional way it would have been upside down on our chassis.
The lesson I'd hand the next steering lead: when two fixed things don't line up, check whether one of them is actually fixed before you design a mechanism to absorb the difference.

08

The fatigue calc I chose not to finish

I ran the Marin equation on the solid shaft and got an endurance limit of 27,026 psi against an applied 44,966 psi — finite life, so I started working out cycles to failure. Then I took it to a design review and got a better answer than the arithmetic.

Steel has a true fatigue limit; the S-N curve flattens into an asymptote, so below it the part effectively lives forever. A steering column's cycle count is set by how often the driver steers — sprung structure cycles around 2.5–3 Hz, unsprung parts like hubs and uprights see 20–30 Hz. The parts worth a deep fatigue study on a Baja car are the 7075 aluminium ones down there, not a steel column up here. On a one-season car, design to a solid factor of safety of 2 and spend the hours somewhere they change the outcome.

Recording it because knowing when to stop calculating is engineering judgement, and it was taught to me rather than invented by me. Von Mises factor of safety on the final column: n ≈ 2.
09

Iterations, and building it

COLUMN CONCEPTS
Three layouts — one U-joint, two U-joints, none — plus four-plus versions of the column itself as diameter, wall thickness, material and support positions moved.
RACK CONCEPTS
Two — a conventional rack, then the NARRco 13T 17.4 MKII racing rack, which matched our ratio and packaging and is built for this duty.
DRIVER-SET DIMENSIONS
Column length landed at 27″ — not the 33.3″ my first geometric estimate gave, but what both drivers preferred sitting in the actual seat. Wheel angle and position came out of the same sessions.
MY HANDS ON IT
Machining, drilling, fixturing, inspection and troubleshooting; NARRco mounting tabs, column-to-rack attachment and spline-shaft-to-wheel assembly. 100+ shop hours, some weeks 8–12 hours a day.

Weld sizing was scoped and then handed over: I started the weld-length work ↗, and our manufacturing lead — who has real welding experience and was laying the beads — judged the full calculation unnecessary for these joints. I stopped rather than duplicate his call.

10

Brakes and ergonomics — where I helped

Officially steering, practically wherever the team was short. On brakes I ran calculations, researched calipers and pads, and worked the master-cylinder decision — Tilton versus Wilwood, settled on Wilwood because it matched our numbers. On the pedal box I drove the ratio from 1:1 to 5:1 and evaluated three-plus return mechanisms.

The failure mode I owned there is unglamorous and real: a pedal that sticks, fails to reach full throttle, or fails to return to idle — and a return spring stiff enough to be quick will slam the pedal into the driver's knees. I trialled several options, from a clothespin-style spring to heavy-duty coils, and picked a linear return element that can be lubricated when it starts to drag. Brake calcs ↗ · clean sheet ↗

On ergonomics the column angle was mine end to end, as was mounting the rack so it stays clear of the bulkhead and the brake lines. The rest of the ergo package belongs to the people who own it.

11

Where it stands, and what I'd change

CAD is released, the column and rack mounts are being built, and validation is next: steering effort at lock against the 50 N·m driver capability, free play at the wheel, and a check that both drivers can reach lock comfortably in the seat. This page gets the test data when it exists.

What I'd do differently: run angle of twist first. I sized the shaft on stress, passed every strength check, and only then found the criterion that actually governed the design — which cost me an iteration. And I'd cut engineering drawings as I went instead of carrying the assembly in CAD and my head; that is the gap in this project I'm least happy about.
